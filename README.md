## Abstract

Governing common-pool resources (CPRs) increasingly requires cooperation across groups under shared-access regimes. In such settings, resource users’ willingness to cooperate depends on their expectations about others’ compliance, including that of in-group and out-group members. A central but understudied question is how information about other groups affects the beliefs and behavioral cues underlying these expectations. We address this question using a framed lab-in-the-field experiment with 164 artisanal fishers from 13 fishing unions in Chile, interacting synchronously across geographically separated coastal communities. Participants played two parallel repeated CPR games with fixed group composition across all 16 rounds: a territorial use rights for fisheries (TURF) game involving only members of their own union, and a Shared Area game involving members of different unions. In Stage 1, participants knew they were interacting with fishers from other unions but not which unions; in Stage 2, out-group union affiliations were revealed, while individual identities remained anonymous. The TURF game provided a parallel comparison across stages. Using structural equation modeling (SEM) of behavioral and self-reported data, we examine how prior beliefs about in-group and out-group compliance and observed compliance relate to individual decisions, and how trust and previous conflict are associated with these beliefs. Average compliance was lower in the Shared Area than in the TURF, but revealing union affiliations was not associated with a statistically significant additional change in compliance relative to the TURF. Consistent with conditional cooperation, individual compliance was positively associated with expected compliance in both stages. When out-group affiliations were unknown, in-group priors and compliance observed in the preceding round were significant informational components, whereas priors about a generic out-group were not. When affiliations were revealed, priors about identified out-groups also became statistically significant, alongside in-group priors; observed compliance retained a positive estimated coefficient that was not statistically significant at the 5% level. Out-group trust was consistently associated with more optimistic out-group priors, while evidence concerning previous conflict was limited. These findings suggest that knowledge of group affiliations can make socially grounded expectations relevant to cooperation, highlighting the importance of pre-existing intergroup relations alongside institutional rules and behavioral information in governing shared resources.

## Repository Contents

The replication package is organized into the following directories:

* **`Ethics/`** — Institutional ethics committee approval documents and informed consent materials for the lab-in-the-field sessions.
* **`Instructions/`** — Experimental instructions, decision interfaces, and survey instruments administered to participants during the TURF and Shared Area CPR games.
* **`Photos/`** — Fieldwork photographs documenting the mobile experimental setup and synchronous sessions across coastal fishing communities.
* **`R/`** — Replication datasets and R scripts to reproduce the descriptive statistics, structural equation models (SEM), tables, and figures reported in the manuscript and supplementary materials.
